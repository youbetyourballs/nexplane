# Post 01: The System Produces Bad Outcomes, Not Bad People

**Phase:** 1 — Establish the Problem
**Status:** Draft
**Target:** Week 1, Post 1

---

Nobody in the room is wrong.

The network team protects uptime — that's their job. The dev team ships features — that's their job. The infrastructure team avoids risky changes — that's rational given how they're measured. The business deprioritizes security work that doesn't have a deadline attached to it — that's how every planning cycle goes.

Everyone is doing their job. The system still produces bad security outcomes.

There's an assumption gap that lives in the white space between teams. Developers assume infrastructure hardened the OS. Infrastructure assumes security reviewed the architecture. Security assumes someone has tested the backups. Nobody's lying. Nobody dropped the ball on purpose. The gap is structural — it belongs to no one, so no one closes it.

And there's a tradeoff that never gets named out loud. In every planning cycle, security initiatives get pushed. Everyone in the room knows it's a risk. Nobody says "we are explicitly accepting this risk today." It stays implicit — which means it was never actually decided. It just drifted.

After four times as a CISO, I stopped being frustrated at the people and started looking at the system.

The people are fine. The system isn't designed to produce security outcomes. It's designed to produce shipping and uptime. Security is what happens when those two things collide with reality.

That's the actual problem. Not bad people. Not bad intentions. A system that was never designed to do this work.
